WCI phone program v3: two scripts, one scheduling rule

Rewritten 2026-09-05 on Jarred's direction: separate the inbound lead-capture call from the scheduled consultation call; inbound leads book a call and are never cold-pitched; price is stated as a floor ($35,000 per session, one to two sessions minimum); the insurance answer is a no with options, never a bare no. 0. Call scheduling law (hard rule)

An inbound lead is someone who filled out the form, chatted, left a voicemail, or called the line. The next step for every inbound lead is a scheduled consultation call at a time the lead picks. Reps do not pitch inbound leads on an unscheduled outbound call. The only outbound call a rep may place to an unbooked lead is a two-minute scheduling call (Script C): confirm they asked for contact, offer times, book, hang up. No facts, no records ask, no pitch on that call. If a lead calls in live and wants to talk now, the rep asks for twenty minutes and runs the consultation script on the spot; a live inbound call is a scheduled call the lead scheduled for right now.

Why this is a hard rule and not a preference: the corpus shows 413 insurance-to-price jumps and a first-call structure that was set by whoever answered, not by the caller. A booked call has a caller who chose the time, has the records question in mind, and has the decision-maker on the line. It also ends the "you called me" complaint class.

Script A: inbound lead capture (form, chat, voicemail, or a caller who cannot talk now)

Goal: know who they are, whether a review is even possible, disclose the three facts so nobody books blind, and book the consultation call. Five minutes.

1. Consent and open. "Thanks for reaching out to Williams Cancer Institute, this is [name]. This line can be recorded for quality; is that okay?" [wait] "I'm sorry you're dealing with this. My job on this call is to get you a time with me or a colleague for a proper conversation, and to make sure you're not wasting a trip. Can I ask you three quick things?"

2. Who. "Am I speaking with the patient, or a family member?" Family members can hear the three facts. Records and the medical conversation need the patient or someone with legal authority on the booked call. "Who should be on that call with you?"

3. Active tumor. "On the most recent scans, was there still a tumor, or was it gone or in remission? If you're not sure, what did the doctor say after the last scan?" Active or unsure: continue. Clear remission: "The team only reviews cases with an active tumor on recent imaging. If that changes, call me; my direct line will be in the email." End warmly. No booking, no records.

4. Three facts, before booking. "Three things now, so the call is worth your time. All treatment is in Mexico; California is administrative. We don't accept insurance; families pay directly, and we'll talk through how on the call. Treatment starts at $35,000 per session, with one to two sessions minimum. I'll pause there." If cost is a hard stop: "Understood. The review stays free if that changes. I won't take up more of your time today." No booking after a cost-no.

5. Book it. "Let's pick a time. Thirty minutes. What day works, and is morning or afternoon better? … [day/time]. I'll send a calendar invite and a short email now with my direct line. Have the most recent scan report and pathology report handy for that call; you don't need to send anything yet."

6. Close. "So: [day/time], you and [who], I call you at [number]. Anything you want me to make sure we cover?" Log the question; it opens the consultation call.

Rules for Script A: no procedure description beyond "the physician injects immunotherapy into the tumor under imaging; the doctor covers the rest"; no records request; no outcome talk; no financing specifics beyond "we'll walk through it."

Script B: the scheduled consultation call (outbound at the booked time)

Goal: the caller states their situation in their own words, the rep answers every direct question from the fact sheet, and the call ends with the authorization signed and the upload started, or a clean no. Thirty minutes.

1. Consent, then their question first. "This is [name] from Williams Cancer Institute for our [time] call; is now still good? This call can be recorded for quality, okay?" [wait] "When we booked, you wanted to make sure we covered [logged question]. Let's start there."

2. Their story, briefly. "Tell me where things stand. What's the diagnosis, what have you done so far, and what did the doctor say after the last scan?" Reflect it back once, without adjectives: "So this is [type], [treatments so far], and the last scans in [month] still showed the tumor. Did I get that right?"

3. What the treatment is, in one line. "The physician injects immunotherapy drugs directly into the tumor under image guidance, sometimes with cryoablation. Which drugs, and whether ablation is added, is the physician's call after reviewing the scans. I won't go further than that; I'm not the doctor."

4. Money, plainly, before any records ask. "Before I ask you to send anything: treatment starts at $35,000 per session, plan on one to two sessions minimum, and the physician sets the number after review. You'll have a written estimate before anything is paid. We don't take insurance, and here's how families handle that…" (Section 4 options, then the pause.) "I'll stop there. What's your reaction?"

- Cost is a hard stop: "Understood. I won't take records if cost is the wall. The review stays free if anything changes." End. No discount, no urgency.

- "Is that per session?": "Yes. One to two minimum; the doctor sets it."

5. Free review and the records handoff, on the call. "If you want the team to look at the case, the review is free. It needs the most recent scans and pathology you have. I'm emailing the authorization and a secure upload link right now; I'll stay on while it lands. If the records are with the hospital, I'll send the release they need and you hand it to them; we can't pull records ourselves." The patient signs the authorization.

6. Checkpoint, stated as effort. "Once the packet is complete I call you when the team has looked. We're aiming for two business days, and I'll tell you if it's slipping. Let's put the packet check on the calendar now: [day/time]."

7. Physician consultation. Booked only after the review. The rep never previews outcomes. Whether it can be virtual depends on the physician's licensure where the patient is; scheduling confirms.

8. Alignment close. "So: I've sent the authorization and the link, you send scans and pathology, I call you [day]. Did I get that right? If you decide this isn't for you, tell me; that's fine."

Empathy in Script B: three beats. The opening sentence, the reflection in step 2, and the pause after money. No "I understand how you feel," no affect labels, no sympathy line before the records ask.

Script C: outbound scheduling call to an unbooked inbound lead (two minutes)

"Hi, this is [name] from Williams Cancer Institute. You asked us to reach out through our website on [day]. I'm only calling to set up a time to talk properly; is there a day this week that works? … [book]. I'll send the invite and my direct line now. Have your latest scan and pathology reports handy for that call. Talk then."

If they want to talk now: "I can do that. Do you have twenty minutes?" Then Script B. If they say they never filled out a form: apologize, remove from the list, end. No facts, no pitch, no second call if they don't book.

4. Insurance: a no with options (the never-a-bare-no rule)

The rep says no once, clearly, then hands the caller a path. The rep names categories and neutral resources; the rep never recommends a lender, quotes terms, helps fill in an application, or suggests borrowing against a house or a life policy. Those are licensed acts (section 2 of the page, sources 13 to 19), and the in-office enrollment model is the one regulators have prosecuted (CareCredit, 2013).

Today, with no partner signed (this works now; nothing to build):

"We don't accept insurance and can't bill it. Families pay directly, and it's a no on insurance, not a no on getting here. Most families use some mix of savings, an unsecured personal loan from a US lender (several of them lend up to $100,000 and pay the money to you, not to us), family help, and sometimes a fundraiser. I'll email a one-page list of the options with the invite. We're not lenders or advisors, so the list points you to the lenders and the government resources, and if you want someone to walk through it with you, tell me and I'll set that up."

The one-page list: consumer-direct lenders (SoFi and LightStream to $100k, Prosper to $50k, United Credit to $25k; the patient applies on the lender's site and WCI has no relationship with them), 401(k)/IRA hardship rules (IRS topic), the HUD reverse-mortgage counseling line (1-800-569-4287) for homeowners 62+, life-policy options via a licensed life-settlement broker or the family's own attorney or CPA, and crowdfunding platforms. Every item says who provides it and that WCI is not a party.

When a written financing partnership exists (needs a product: a US lender willing to fund cross-border care, a written no-compensation or disclosed-compensation term, and a page that routes to the lender's own application):

"We don't accept insurance. We've partnered with [lender]; you apply on their site and they set the terms, not us. Want me to include the link in the email?" The fertility and rehab pages in the brief use exactly this shape: name the lender, "partnered with," application on the lender's site, clinic role limited to coordinating.

A financial navigator: WCI can hire or contract one today as a neutral resource role (ACCC trains financial advocates; the title is not licensed). The role stops at the resource list and the referral to licensed people. Provider-enrolled cards (CareCredit, Cherry, Alphaeon) are a no for now: the only Mexico source says they require US providers, and their fee and enforcement history argue against them anyway.

Never: "insurance might reimburse," "you could take out a reverse mortgage," "you can't lose your home," "no payments," a specific APR or monthly figure, a lender recommendation for any incentive, filling in an application for a family, shaping a fundraiser's story, "financing" or "payment plan" as a WCI product when none exists, any urgency tied to money.

6. Never say

success rate, percentage, "most patients" · guarantee, cure, remission prediction · "no side effects," "minimally invasive" as a safety answer · any pathology statistic · "FDA approved / cleared / registered" · stop, delay or replace current treatment · disparaging standard care · interpreting scans or pathology · "you qualify / you're a candidate / you're a fit" · "we treat all types / all stages" · any price below $35,000 or a course figure the doctor hasn't set · "insurance might cover / reimburse" · "financing" or "payment plan" without a ratified product · "reverse mortgage," "home equity," "borrow against" as a suggestion · a specific APR or monthly payment · "the consultation is virtual" as a promise · stay length or duration before ratification · pulling hospital records ourselves · drug brand names · urgency or scarcity · "I understand how you feel" · pitching on an unscheduled outbound call.

7. Test before rollout

Four to six weeks, randomized by call within rep. Primary: physician consultations booked per 100 inbound leads. Gate: complaint, refund-request and attorney-contact rate non-inferior. Secondaries: booked-call show rate; price disclosed before the first records ask; authorization signed on the call; packet complete at checkpoint; price-surprise callbacks (caller believed one session was the course); third-press outcome breaks; unscheduled outbound pitches (should be zero).

2. Oncology financial navigation

  • The role. ACCC: advocates help patients "navigate insurance coverage, manage out-of-pocket costs, and access financial assistance resources"; toolkit tasks are "screening for financial distress, optimizing insurance coverage, performing prior authorizations," "handling denials and appeals" [12 E]. AONN+: the navigator addresses "the patient's financial burden relative to their care" [11 E].
  • Training. ACCC Financial Advocacy Boot Camp for those "experienced" or "new to the field"; not called a certification; no cost/format listed [12]. AONN+ credentials (OPN-CG) are general navigation, not financial [11]. "Association of Oncology Financial Navigators" / "Certified Oncology Financial Navigator": nothing surfaced, not verified. Family Reach, Triage Cancer, PAF, CancerCare: not fetched.
  • Can a private clinic hire one? Nothing fetched restricts the title; it is a job role, not a licensed profession (inference from [11][12]). But the substance is insurance-centric [12] and nearly none of it exists for cash-pay, out-of-country, non-FDA-reviewed care. At WCI it collapses into "how you pay us" counseling, what Hope4Cancer's admissions team does ("Speak with the admissions team for a list of trusted lending partners") [20].
  • Limit. Once that person helps a family choose or apply for a loan, tap a policy, or pull home equity for compensation, §3 licensing attaches.

3. Rules the rep or navigator must respect

HECM reverse mortgage. Borrower "62 and older"; "principal residence"; "You must receive counseling from a HUD-approved reverse mortgage counseling agency" covering "eligibility, the financial implications of the loan, and other alternatives" [13 P]. Lender must give the counselor list "at the time of the initial contact"; counselor must probe any "estate planning service firm" fee contract; borrower delivers the certificate [14 P, 24 CFR 206.41]. FTC: "Some salespeople try to rush you through the process"; some pressure you "to buy other financial products"; "You don't have to buy any financial products, services, or investment to get a reverse mortgage... in some situations, it's illegal to insist that you buy other products"; counseling "often around $125" [15 P, Aug 2022].

Who may originate or steer. SAFE Act: a state must license anyone who "in a commercial context and habitually or repeatedly" takes a residential mortgage application and "offers or negotiates terms... for compensation or gain", which expressly includes one who "recommends, refers, or steers a borrower... to a particular lender... in accordance with a duty to or incentive from any person other than the borrower" and "receives or expects to receive... anything of value" [16 P, 12 CFR 1008.103]. Reg Z's mortgage "loan originator" covers anyone who, for compensation, "assists a consumer in obtaining or applying to obtain... an extension of consumer credit," excluding only "purely administrative or clerical tasks" [17 P, 12 CFR 1026.36(a)(1)]. HELOCs are residential mortgage loans, so the same test applies. CFPB fined three reverse-mortgage advertisers ($400k/$325k/$65k, Dec 2016) for "could not lose their home," "no payments," implied government affiliation and false urgency, under the Mortgage Acts and Practices Advertising Rule and Dodd-Frank's deception ban [18 P].

Life settlements / viaticals (California). "A person may not enter into, broker, or solicit life settlements... unless that person has been licensed by the commissioner"; 15 CE hours before brokering; exemption only for an attorney, CPA or accredited planner "retained to represent the owner" and not paid by the buyer [19 P, Ins. Code §10113.2]. Accelerated-death-benefit riders not fetched; Hope4Cancer's "Living Benefit Loan" referral is a third-party loan against the policy ("up to 50%" of death benefit, "$75,000 of coverage" minimum) [20].

401(k) hardship. Only if the plan permits; "immediate and heavy financial need," "limited to the amount necessary"; "medical care expenses" are safe-harbor; "subject to income taxes," "may also be subject to a 10% additional tax"; cannot be repaid or rolled over [21 P].

Medical cards. No licensing analog fetched, but the CareCredit order shows the exposure: the remedy was that "for certain transactions of more than $1,000, consumers will enroll directly through a CareCredit representative," not office staff [9].

Line for an unlicensed WCI employee (from [15][16][17][19]): may say these products exist, hand over neutral resources (HUD line, IRS topic, lender URL), and say "we are not lenders or advisors." May not recommend a lender for any incentive, present or compare terms, help complete a mortgage/HELOC application, solicit a policy sale, promise "you can't lose your home," or create urgency.

4. Risk and precedent

  • CFPB v. GE Capital / CareCredit (2013-12-10): up to $34.1M refunds, >1M consumers; 175,000+ enrolled providers; "office staff had received little or no training... and relied only on pamphlets"; ~85% of borrowers on 26.99% deferred interest. Remedies: mandatory staff training, plain-language disclosures, 72-hour follow-up calls, direct enrollment for certain >$1,000 transactions [9 P].
  • CFPB report, May 2023: "65 percent of medical credit cards are signed in medical providers' offices"; "$1 billion in deferred interest... from 2018-2020"; 20% of healthcare promo purchases incurred deferred interest (34% below score 619); "medical providers have an additional incentive to enroll as many patients as possible" [10 P].
  • Reverse-mortgage ad actions, Dec 2016: §3 [18].
  • Crowdfunding for Tijuana clinics (Snyder, Zenone, Grewal, Caulfield; JMIR Cancer, 2024-08-14): GoFundMe/GiveSendGo campaigns Jan 2022–Feb 2023 "raised US $5,275,268.37"; campaigns "spread problematic misinformation about the likely efficacy of CAM treatments, funnel money and endorsements to CAM clinics in Tijuana, and leave many campaigners short of the money needed" [22 P abstract]. Clinic shares (Hope4Cancer/CHIPSA/Oasis >75%) appeared only in search snippets, not verified.
  • What a Tijuana clinic publishes (Hope4Cancer, fetched 2026-09-05): crowdfunding, with the clinic offering to "help guide you in preparing your campaign story"; early IRA/401(k) withdrawals; Canadian/Australian retirement release; a Life Credit "Living Benefit Loan" via a clinic-specific URL; a named Abacus Life Settlements contact; LendingTree and SoFi. 3. Research brief: what the evidence supports

Recommendation (high-confidence findings only)

Resting only on HIGH-confidence findings: (1) Structure every inbound call to end with a specific, mutually agreed next action set at both the start (up-front contract, 'no is fine') and the end; treat a vague 'continuation' as a failed call. (2) Investigate before presenting: get the caller to state an explicit need in their own words (3-4 problems, deep not wide) before describing the program. (3) Use the NURSE/ask-tell-ask toolkit with its phrasing rules (suggestive naming, intensity down a notch, truthful non-abandonment statements) to catch and respond to the emotional cues that clinicians miss 78-90% of the time; expect it to reduce distress and modestly raise trust, NOT to drive conversion by itself, and do not let relational talk substitute for competent answers to direct questions. (4) Model the records stage on the cancer-center primary sources: identity, diagnosis, how/where diagnosed, referring physician and release-of-information on call one, with a published callback and appointment SLA (2 business days / 5-7 days), and use the 11-18% pathology re-diagnosis rate as the honest reason to request slides. (5) Do NOT adopt the Tijuana clinics' safety, efficacy or cost-comparison framings verbatim: FTC, CDC, CRUK and peer-reviewed crowdfunding data contradict them and create liability; adopt only their neutral structural moves (range price with inclusions, plain insurance answer plus itemized invoices, coordinator-scoped travel logistics). (6) Never use urgency or pressure closes; they are documented scam red flags in this vertical. Evidence is INSUFFICIENT to recommend a specific price-disclosure minute-mark, question count, talk ratio, or any records/consult conversion benchmark; those must be self-measured, and the first-call-money and validate-and-clarify objection patterns should be treated as medium-confidence directional guidance pending the clinic's own call data.

Findings (27)

  1. [high] Communication-skills training in oncology reliably changes clinician behavior (more empathic statements, more open questions) but produces only small patient-outcome effects: Tulsky 2011 RCT trust +0.1 on a 5-point scale (P=0.036) with no significant change in perceived empathy or alliance; Cochrane 2018 pooled SMD 0.18 for demonstrated empathy (moderate certainty) but no evidence of benefit on patient satisfaction, anxiety, or burnout; Howick 2018 SMD -0.18 across pain/anxiety/satisfaction. Design an empathy layer for the call, but do not promise it will move conversion by itself. (8 claims)
  2. [high] Clinicians miss most empathic opportunities: oncologists gave empathic 'continuer' responses to only 22% of opportunities (Pollak 2007) and 10% in Morse 2008, with half of empathic statements crammed into the last third of the visit; patients hint rather than state emotion and escalate when ignored (Suchman 1997). The fixable failure is not lack of warmth but failing to notice and respond in the moment. (4 claims)
  3. [high] The canonical, source-quoted empathy toolkit is NURSE + ask-tell-ask + 'tell me more' (Back 2005, VitalTalk), with specific phrasing rules: name emotions suggestively not declaratively ('I wonder if you're feeling...' not 'I can see you're angry'), turn the intensity down a notch, stop short of claiming full understanding ('I cannot imagine what it is like'), and make non-abandonment commitments only if truthful ('I'll be with you during this illness, no matter what happens'). Five named pitfalls: blocking, lecturing, routine-dependence, collusion, premature reassurance. Note the whole NURSE/Oncotalk/VitalTalk evidence base is one author lineage (Back/Arnold/Tulsky). (10 claims)
  4. [medium] A ~40-second scripted compassion insert (Fogarty 1999: 'I know this is a tough experience to go through and I want you to know that I am here with you... We are here together, and we will go through this together') lowered post-visit state anxiety (40.0 vs 44.7, P=.011) in a randomized video study, but did NOT improve information recall (recall was worse) or change treatment decisions. Compassion language is cheap and anxiety-reducing; it is not a persuasion device. (5 claims)
  5. [medium] Empathy transfers to phone and video: nurse telephone follow-up was equivalent to clinic follow-up on anxiety with higher satisfaction (Beaver 2009 RCT, n=374); a 2022 rapid review found empathy is conveyable by phone but 'does not yet identify how' with half of studies at high risk of bias; qualitative video-visit work shows rapport is built by deliberate small talk, remembering personal details, and exaggerated nonverbal cues. Patients still prefer in-person for bad news and first encounters. (5 claims)
  6. [medium] No fetched randomized trial trained clinic phone/intake staff in NURSE/SPIKES-style empathy and measured conversion to a booked or kept oncology appointment. The only appointment-conversion evidence is from adjacent contexts: telephone follow-up nearly doubled screening-trial attendance vs mail (17.5% vs 9.1%, RR 1.92), and telephone motivational interviewing raised cancer-screening uptake with mixed results for single-contact MI. One contradicting signal: in a quitline study, increased empathy alone decreased quit attempts. (5 claims)
  7. [medium] SPIKES supplies the bad-news phrasing bank (warning shots 'I'm sorry to tell you that...', empathic 'I can see how upsetting this is to you', exploratory 'Tell me more about it', validating 'Many other patients have had a similar experience') but its authors concede it is not fully derived from empirical data and its only reported outcome is clinician confidence. (5 claims)
  8. [high] All four major sales methodologies plus Gong's call data converge on ending every call with a specific committed next action: SPIN treats an Advance as success and a Continuation as failure; Sandler sets an up-front contract at both the start and end of the call ('no' is an acceptable outcome); Gong finds successful reps spend 12.7% more time on next steps and the fastest deals spent 53% more time on next steps in the first meeting. (6 claims)
  9. [high] Investigation precedes presentation in SPIN, Sandler and Gap Selling: SPIN found Explicit Needs (customer-stated) predict success while Implied Needs do not, the product should be presented only after an Explicit Need is voiced, and continual closing techniques reduce success outside petty-cash sales. Challenger is the one methodology that leads with insight instead of questions, and Gong's C-suite data (4 questions in successful exec meetings vs 8 in unsuccessful) partially supports it for senior buyers. (9 claims)
  10. [medium] Discovery should go deep on 3-4 problems, not wide: Gong (519,291 calls) found 3-4 problems maximizes advancement and more problems dilutes urgency; questions should be spread through the call rather than front-loaded. Question-count guidance is internally inconsistent (11-14 in the older study vs 15-16 for won deals in the 2025 re-analysis), so treat the count as loose and the depth rule as the durable one. Sandler's official Pain Funnel supplies the depth sequence ('Tell me more about that... How long has that been a problem?... What have you tried?... How much do you think that has cost you?... how do you feel about that?'). (5 claims)
  11. [medium] Money belongs on the FIRST call, after value is built: Gong reports win rates of 42% when pricing is discussed on call one vs 32% on call two and 5% never, and 49% vs 7% for budget; top performers raise price in roughly the 38-49 minute window of long-form calls, mention it 3-4 times, and keep the explanation to a minute or two. Sandler agrees money is first-call but elicits the BUYER's budget (with a bracket: 'most of the time... the total investment falls somewhere in the range of $X to $Y. Do you think the amount you would be available to invest would fall within that range?') BEFORE presenting. Gong's own first-call-length study found no length-to-advancement correlation and favors 30-minute invites, so the minute-marks are relative positions, not a call-length prescription. (14 claims)
  12. [medium] Peer-reviewed pricing psychology supports value-before-price for desirability-driven purchases (Karmarkar et al. 2015: price-first shifts evaluation to monetary worth and only helps bargain items) and shows first offers anchor final settlements (Galinsky & Mussweiler 2001) unless the counterpart focuses on alternatives or their own target. Both are lab/retail studies, so application to $30k+ medical consults is extrapolation. (4 claims)
  13. [medium] Talk ratio matters less than folklore claims: Gong's 2016 '43:57' and the widely repeated 46:54 gap shrank to 57% (won) vs 62% (lost) in the 2025 refresh, which the author calls 'not massive'; the stronger signal is that high performers keep a stable ratio while low performers swing 10 points. Chorus reports converting cold calls at 44% rep talk time. Target roughly 50-60% listening and consistency rather than a magic number. (4 claims)
  14. [medium] A peer-reviewed B2C study (Singh et al. 2017, JAMS, 32 simulated life-insurance interactions) found that warmth/emoting behaviors during customer QUERY handling curtailed or neutralized the positive effect of competence (resolving) behaviors on interest; queries should be answered as resolving opportunities, not softened with relational talk. This is a tension with, not a refutation of, the NURSE/Sandler emotional-labeling guidance, which targets emotion moments rather than information queries. (4 claims)
  15. [high] Nearly all quantitative sales-call figures are vendor-proprietary correlational data: 15 of 26 high-ticket sources are gong.io (one dataset family, not independent corroboration), Gong itself cautions correlation is not causation, and the Huthwaite 10%→23% and Challenger profile figures are vendor marketing. Use them as directional priors, not effect sizes. (4 claims)
  16. [medium] Healthcare lead-to-booking baselines are mediocre and staff-dependent: Liine reports 39.9% of marketing-sourced leads (278k) and 54% of all inquiries (600k+) ever book, with up to 60% variation between individual staff; Invoca reports 40% in-call conversion in healthcare; Patient Prism claims 30-40% industry standard and 52.36% for its top clients. All are vendor datasets with no methodology disclosure; the staff-variation figure is the most useful because it implies training/scripting headroom. (6 claims)
  17. [high] Cancer-center intake is a records-gated process with published SLAs: Roswell Park calls back within 2 business days and targets a second-opinion appointment within 5 days; Karmanos navigators call within 24-48 hours, handle the release-of-information and insurance verification on that first call, and offer an appointment within 7 days; Nebraska Medicine requires records BEFORE scheduling; Fox Chase's first call collects identity, diagnosis, how/where diagnosed, and referring physician. Pathology re-review changes the diagnosis in 11-18% of Roswell cases, which is a legitimate, non-salesy reason to ask for slides. (8 claims)
  18. [high] Consult-to-treatment conversion in adjacent high-ticket elective verticals is roughly 50-70% for eligible patients in peer-reviewed cohorts (bariatric: 71.2% orientation→office visit, 50.8% office visit→surgery; 70% of eligible candidates operated within 2 years) with 53-60% overall funnel dropout driven mainly by wait times (median wait rose from 86 to 159 days) and pre-op requirements; leakage to competitors happens early, before committee review. Fertility peer-reviewed baseline is far lower (8.8-14.2% new-visit→IVF) and a personalized prognosis report roughly tripled it (with author COI). (8 claims)
  19. [medium] No fetched source in any of six verticals publishes a call→records or records→consult benchmark; cosmetic (35-50% median, 75-85% top), addiction (VOB→admit 10% vs 25%+), and medical-tourism (10% inquiry→patient target) figures are consultant/agency/trade-press claims without samples, and vendor speed-to-lead figures ('9x within 5 minutes', '15-30% lost per 24h') are unsourced folklore. Any benchmark the clinic adopts for the records stage will have to be self-measured. (8 claims)
  20. [medium] Documented first-call playbooks across verticals share a common skeleton: capture identity and contact first, screen candidacy/severity on call one, initiate insurance/benefits verification on the call rather than 'we'll call you back', collect or arrange records, set expectations for the journey, book the next step before hanging up, and run a multi-touch follow-up cadence (medical tourism: 2nd email within 48h, 2nd call within 4-7 days, sign-off by day 7-10; cosmetic: same-day call, SMS at 24-48h, value-add at 3-5 days, close-out at 1-2 weeks). Voxjar's addiction QA scorecard operationalizes this as gradeable items. (11 claims)
  21. [high] The Tijuana alternative-cancer clinics (ITC, Hope4Cancer, Oasis of Hope, CHIPSA) handle the hard objections with a consistent FAQ pattern: price stated as a range with inclusions ($18,995 for 3 weeks at ITC; $45,000-60,000 at Hope4Cancer; Oasis defers a quote to a coordinator and claims 40-60% below US cost; CHIPSA calls its price an estimate); insurance answered 'unfortunately we cannot process claims' plus payment methods or third-party funding; success rates deflected to 'depends on your case' or 'averages don't apply to you'; safety answered with neighborhood scoping (Playas de Tijuana), border proximity, escorted transport and 24-hour security; conventional treatment answered 'complementary, if you wish'. None contains a 'my oncologist says no' or 'is this a scam' question. (22 claims)
  22. [high] Those clinic FAQ patterns carry real regulatory and reputational exposure: FTC requires randomized controlled human testing for efficacy claims and says testimonials with 'results not typical' disclaimers do not cure deception; CDC calls medical travel 'can be risky' and names Mexico as the top US destination; Cancer Research UK states there is no evidence alternative therapies treat cancer and professional therapists do not claim cures; peer-reviewed crowdfunding data show these four clinics dominate 360 Tijuana campaigns (Hope4Cancer 40.6%), 78.6% of stated-stage patients were stage 4, 18.6% died after the campaign began, and campaigns raised a median $7,685 against a $45,000 median ask with only 6.1% reaching goal. ITC's own blog and FAQ contradict each other on insurance acceptance. (10 claims)
  23. [medium] For the 'my oncologist says no' objection, the only defensible documented pattern is the integrative/compliant one: 'Absolutely not, do not stop your conventional treatment; we coordinate with your oncologist' (GWCIM), PlacidWay's 'do not stop, delay or change chemotherapy unless your treating oncologist directs it', and CRUK/AMA guidance that the patient should discuss with their cancer doctor and that physicians must state whether they will provide follow-up care. The sales-side reframing documented by Al Jazeera (casting oncologists as cold-hearted, presenting refusal of chemo as the path to cure) and the crowdfunding 'fighter' narrative are the pattern to avoid. (6 claims)
  24. [medium] For 'is this a scam', no rep-side medical-tourism script exists in fetched sources; the transferable pattern is the insurance/B2B trust script (validate the question, cite verifiable third-party proof, offer to show it) combined with supplying exactly what patient-side guides tell prospects to demand: surgeon full name and license number for medical-board lookup, accreditation verifiable on JCI/DNV/ISQua sites, an itemised written cost breakdown before payment, a reasonable deposit rather than full upfront payment, and card payment. Urgency language ('only two spots left', 'price valid 24 hours'), guarantees, crypto/messaging-app deposits, and hiding price behind a phone call are named scam red flags. (9 claims)
  25. [medium] For 'I need to think about it', two script families exist: pressure closes (Sales Gravy #4/#5: 'Thinking about it more won't fix things for you – only making a decision will') and validate-and-clarify ('Totally fair. Is it the fit, the timing, or the investment you want to think through?'; 'what new information do you expect to have in 30 days?'; lock a concrete follow-up day and time). Because medical-tourism consumer guidance explicitly flags time pressure as a scam signal and says legitimate hospitals encourage patients to take time, only the validate-and-clarify family is usable in a cancer-clinic context. (8 claims)
  26. [medium] For price/affordability, high-ticket and medical scripts agree on three moves: separate affordability from perceived value in one question ('Is it that the number is out of reach right now, or that you're not yet sure it's worth it?'; 'If budget weren't a factor at all, how do you feel about what we've put together?'), never discount but offer payment structure or a smaller scope, and return to the patient's stated goal/cost-of-inaction rather than defending the fee. Medical front-desk scripts also refuse to diagnose or quote by phone and convert a price-shopper into a free/second-opinion visit, while forbidding misleading callers to get them in the door. (10 claims)
  27. [medium] For 'insurance not accepted', the documented compliant patterns are: state plainly that direct billing is not possible, explain why (outside the US / private facility), offer itemized invoices and reports for the patient's own reimbursement attempt, redirect to financing/third-party funding, and refocus on the patient's goal; PlacidWay adds the patient-protective step of getting the insurer's decision in writing. A dental analogue offers a complimentary benefits analysis at the visit rather than promising coverage. (6 claims)
Contradictions in the evidence (17)
  • Gong question-count: 11-14 questions per discovery call (older study) vs 15-16 for won deals and ~20 for lost deals (2025 re-analysis of 326K calls). Same vendor, different samples; use the ordering (fewer than lost deals, spread throughout) not the number. [high-ticket-structure-037 vs -038]
  • Gong talk-to-listen ratio: 43:57 (2016) and 46:54 (widely repeated) vs 57% talk won / 62% lost, overall 60:40 (2025), with the author calling the gap 'not massive'. [high-ticket-structure-040 vs -041]
  • Gong pricing window: 40-49 minutes (25,537-call study) vs 38-46 minutes in other Gong posts and 'minutes 40-45' in a chart caption; and the whole window presupposes 45-60 minute calls while Gong's own first-call study found no length-to-advancement correlation and favors 30-minute invites. [high-ticket-structure-043, -044, -056, -057]
  • Money sequencing: Sandler elicits the buyer's budget BEFORE presenting (step 4 of 7, pre-Fulfillment) while Gong puts the seller's price disclosure at the END of the first call after value is built; both agree money is first-call, they differ on whether budget is elicited before price is stated. [high-ticket-structure-021/-026 vs -046/-047]
  • Warmth vs competence: Singh et al. 2017 (JAMS) found emoting/relating behaviors neutralize competence effects during customer query handling, cutting against Sandler/Gong/NURSE guidance to layer emotional labeling into discovery; scope differs (query handling in B2C insurance vs emotion moments in oncology), so this is a tension, not a refutation. [high-ticket-structure-068/-071 vs empathy-evidence-033/-040]
  • Challenger leads with insight rather than questions, directly opposite to SPIN/Sandler/Gap Selling which open with investigation; Gong's C-suite data (4 questions in successful exec meetings vs 8) partially supports Challenger for senior buyers. [high-ticket-structure-028/-030 vs -004/-033; -055]
  • Communication training outcomes: Tulsky 2011 found increased trust (small) and Howick 2018 found small anxiety/satisfaction benefits, while Cochrane 2018 found no evidence of benefit on patient satisfaction or anxiety and Curtis 2013 (JAMA, n=472 trainees) found no improvement in patient-reported communication quality and HIGHER depression scores in patients of trained clinicians. [empathy-evidence-007/-022 vs -021/-026]
  • Compassion and recall: Fogarty 1999 enhanced-compassion group had lower anxiety but WORSE information recall and no change in decisions. [empathy-evidence-017 vs -018]
  • Empathy alone can hurt: Klemperer quitline study found working alliance raised quit attempts but increased empathy alone decreased them. [empathy-evidence-046 vs -022]
  • Pollak 2007 figures: IOM summary reports empathic statements in 11% of conversations while the abstract reports 22% continuer responses when an opportunity occurred; different denominators. [empathy-evidence-056]
  • Bariatric seminar format: Surg Endosc 2022 found no seminar-type effect on surgery (52% online vs 50.2% in-person, p=0.339) while Obesity Surgery 2018 found higher attrition to surgery among in-house (72.9%) than online (66.6%) attendees; denominators differ (office-visit attendees vs all seminar attendees). [clinic-intake-playbooks-22 vs -27]
  • Rehab funnel benchmarks: Ads Up gives inquiry→VOB 30% industry / 55%+ high-performing and VOB→admit 10% / 25%+, while Web Tonic gives lead→VOB 25-40% 'healthy' and viable→admit 33-54%; stage definitions ('inquiry' vs 'viable') differ and none disclose samples. [clinic-intake-playbooks-39 vs -45]
  • Fertility conversion: consultancy scale (20% very poor to 70% very good) vs peer-reviewed multicenter baseline of 8.8-14.2% new-visit→IVF; the numbers are not comparable (retrievals/new-patients ratio vs patient-level conversion) and the peer-reviewed intervention arm has author COI. [clinic-intake-playbooks-14/-15 vs -16/-17]
  • ITC insurance: its blog says 'Hospitals may also accept your U.S. health insurance coverage' while its FAQ says most major insurers do not cover alternative treatments. [objection-handling-insurance-02]
  • Clinic safety framing ('as safe as anywhere else', 'no outsized risk', 'Yes' it is safe) vs CDC 'traveling to another country to get medical care can be risky' with infection, resistant-organism, post-surgery clot and continuity-of-care risks, and CRUK warnings that stopping treatment for unproven therapy can harm health. [objection-handling-travel-01/-03/-05 vs -07/-09]
  • Clinic efficacy framing ('often more effective and have fewer side effects') vs CRUK 'no scientific evidence that alternative therapies can treat people with cancer', FTC substantiation requirements, and peer-reviewed finding that Tijuana CAM campaigns spread problematic efficacy misinformation. [objection-handling-success-02 vs -12/-13/-08]
  • Urgency-based 'think about it' closes (Sales Gravy #4/#5) vs medical-tourism consumer guidance treating 'limited time only' and 'price valid 24 hours' language as scam red flags and stating legitimate hospitals encourage patients to take time. [objection-handling-think-03 vs -10, scam-08, scam-10]

Rules from the evidence: end every call with a specific, mutually agreed next action (SPIN and Sandler prescribe it; Gong reports an association with advancement — high confidence on the practice, correlational on the effect); investigate before presenting (SPIN: explicit needs predict success); disclose price on the first call after value is built and BEFORE records (Gong: 42% win rate when price is discussed on call one vs 5% never — an association; Sandler's budget bracket is a different move, eliciting the buyer's number, which this script does NOT do); empathy as NURSE/ask-tell-ask beats at emotion moments, with answers to direct questions given first (Singh 2017 as a boundary condition); no urgency or scarcity language (a scam red flag in this vertical).

1. Open — recording notice + acknowledgment + permission (today: announcement spoken in 0.3% of calls)

"Thanks for calling Williams Cancer Institute, this is [first name]. This call is recorded. Before anything else — I'm sorry you're dealing with this. I'll be straight with you about whether we're even a fit, and it's completely fine if the answer is no. Can I ask a few questions first?"

Empathy beat 1 (open): one sentence, then permission. Banned: "I understand", "I know how you feel".

2. Gate A — who am I speaking with, and can they act?

"Am I speaking with the patient, or with a family member?" → relative: "Are you the person who'd be able to send records and make decisions with them, or should we set a time when they can join?"

Branch: no authority → set the next call with the patient/decision-maker; do not pitch.

3. Gate B — active tumor routing (never declare candidacy)

"So I point you the right way: is there an active tumor on recent scans, or is this in remission / no evidence of disease right now?"
  • Active: "Okay. What type of cancer, and roughly when were the last scans?" (collect type, stage if volunteered, last imaging date — nothing more)
  • NED / remission: "The team only reviews cases with an active, measurable tumor on recent imaging. If that's not the situation right now, we wouldn't have a case to review. If imaging later shows an active tumor, call us back — I'll send you my direct line." (end warmly; no records)

Empathy beat 2 (diagnosis loop, NURSE 'understand' + reflect): "So this is [type], [active / recurrent], and the last scans were [when]. Did I get that right? … That's a lot to be carrying."

4. One-line procedure (present after they've told you what they need)

"Here's what we do, in one line: minimally invasive, image-guided intratumoral immunotherapy — the tumor itself is injected with a combination of immunotherapy drugs, sometimes together with cryoablation. What the physician would actually propose depends on your scans."

Only answer what was asked; no mechanism claims beyond this line; no "our treatment works".

5. The three facts, before any records ask (deliberation #1b: disclosure, not a budget screen)

"Before I ask you to send anything, three things you should know now rather than later:
one — all treatment is in Mexico, Los Cabos or Mexico City; our California office is administrative only;
two — we don't accept insurance; treatment is paid out of pocket, and I won't promise reimbursement;
three — cost. Is it okay if I give you the range now?" (wait) "Each session is $35,000 to $75,000 depending on the case, and patients typically need three to six. That's the full range — I can't narrow it before the doctor reviews your records. I'd rather you know that now."

Empathy beat 3 (permission before price + pause after): "I'll give you a second with that. Is this a range you'd want to keep talking about?"

  • If out of reach: "Understood — thank you for telling me. If that changes, the review stays free. Is there anything else I can answer?" (no discounting, no pressure, no scarcity)
  • If "is that per session?": "Yes — per session. Three to six is typical; the doctor sets it."

6. Free case review → assisted records handoff (the conversion lever)

"If you'd like the team to look at your case, the review is free. It needs your most recent scans, the pathology report, and any recent records. Two ways: I email you a consent form and a secure upload link right now and stay on the line while it arrives, or if the records are with a hospital, I can help you request them."

Empathy beat 4 (name the barrier): "Getting records out of a hospital is its own job. Which system is the patient treated in? … Let's do the consent form now while we're on the phone."

  • Do the consent + upload link on this call, not "I'll email you later" (playbook pattern: initiate on-call; the data shows "email" is the #1 fact in records answers because reps defer).
  • Why slides matter, honestly: "A second look at pathology changes the diagnosis in roughly one in ten cases at major centers — that's why the team wants the actual report, not a summary."

7. Navigator checkpoint + SLA (the "advance", not a "continuation")

"Here's what happens next: once the packet is complete, the tumor board reviews it and I call you within two business days with what they need or with a consultation time — usually within a week. Let's put the packet-completion check on the calendar now: [day/time]. If you don't have everything by then, we'll sort out what's missing together."

8. Physician consultation — only after review; the rep books it, never previews outcomes.

9. Close — teach-back

"Before we hang up, can you tell me back the three things you're doing, so I know I explained it right? … Perfect. My direct line is on the email. If you decide it's not for you, that's completely fine — just let me know."

E. Empathy — what the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

  • Communication-skills training reliably changes clinician behaviour (more empathic statements) but the patient-outcome effects are small: trust +0.1/5 (Tulsky 2011); Cochrane 2018 found no satisfaction/anxiety benefit; Howick 2018's small pooled effect is broader, non-oncology evidence. Design the beats; do not expect empathy to move the 11% by itself — the conversion levers are price-before-records and the on-call records handoff.
  • Clinicians miss 78–90% of empathic opportunities (Pollak 2007, Morse 2008); the fixable failure is noticing and responding in the moment — hence four short beats at fixed moments rather than a warm tone throughout.
  • NURSE phrasing rules: name suggestively ("I wonder if this feels…"), intensity one notch down, never claim full understanding, non-abandonment only if truthful ("my direct line stays open either way").
  • Warmth during information queries can neutralise competence (Singh 2017): answer the question first, then the beat.
  • Scripted compassion inserts lower anxiety but worsen recall (Fogarty 1999): keep beats short and follow with the teach-back close.
